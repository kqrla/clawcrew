# strategy: breadth, depth, thin

three moves, one test each. the test is a question a gate can answer from rows, not a judgement call.

## breadth

**the move**: add a slice, a room, a surface, a SKU.

**the test**: does every existing surface hold a depth claim with an external metric? if one does
not, this is not breadth, it is thin.

**what it buys**: a catalog worth returning to, a story, a market that notices. real, and the reason
this is not banned.

**what it costs**: each new surface costs support, documentation, and one more thing that can break
at 3am. meenible's ten slices each carry that cost already.

## depth

**the move**: same surface, better. a harness that catches more, a part that prints reliably, a doc
page that answers the question people actually ask.

**the test**: does the surface's metric move *and* does its support volume fall? depth that does not
reduce cost or increase the number is churn with better branding.

**what it buys**: margin, reliability, and the evidence that makes the next breadth move legitimate.

**what it costs**: visibility. nobody sees the fourth week of depth work, which is why self-directed
orgs skip it.

## thin

**the move**: breadth without depth. usually three of them in a month.

**the test**: two or more surfaces added in the same period with no metric movement on the existing
ones. that is the signature, and it is visible in the ledger before anyone feels it.

**the correction**: freeze the new surface's objectives and spend the next period on depth. not
because breadth was wrong, but because it was early.

## the ordering rule, stated once

```
breadth is allowed only where every existing surface holds a depth claim with an external metric
```

a "depth claim" is an objective whose metric moved by more than the noise floor in the current
period, corroborated by a witness, and whose support or cost signal moved the right way. anything
weaker is a plan, and a plan is never a capability.
